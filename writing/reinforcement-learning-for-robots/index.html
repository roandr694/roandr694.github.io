Notes on reinforcement learning in robotics

Working through the control loop, rewards, value functions, PPO, and simulation-to-hardware transfer.

I find RL easier to follow when I start with the controller. At each timestep, some sensor measurements come in, a command goes out, and the robot moves. Learning changes how that command is chosen. The rest of the system still has to work: the motors, the estimator, the timing, and whatever limits sit between the policy and the hardware.

These are my notes on how the pieces fit together. I am using a small reaching example for the math, with a few references to locomotion and manipulation along the way.

The control loop

At time ttt, the robot observes something, chooses an action, and changes the world. The next observation comes from that changed world. This feedback is what makes the problem different from predicting labels in a fixed dataset.

For a robot arm, the state might include joint positions qqq, velocities q˙\dot qq˙​, object pose, and contact conditions. An observation could be joint encoders plus camera images. The action might be torque, velocity, or a desired joint position passed to a lower-level controller. Those choices define very different learning problems.

For me, the action interface and the control timestep need to be clear before the algorithm discussion. Sending joint-position targets to a low-level controller gives the policy a different job from sending raw torques.

States, actions, and return

A discounted Markov decision process is described by

M=(S,A,p,r,ρ0,γ).\mathcal M=(\mathcal S,\mathcal A,p,r,\rho_0,\gamma).M=(S,A,p,r,ρ0​,γ).

Here p(st+1∣st,at)p(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)p(st+1​∣st​,at​) describes transitions, r(st,at,st+1)r(s_t,a_t,s_{t+1})r(st​,at​,st+1​) is the reward, and ρ0\rho_0ρ0​ is the initial-state distribution. A policy chooses actions according to at∼πθ(⋅∣st)a_t\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)at​∼πθ​(⋅∣st​).

My objective over a finite episode is

J(θ)=Eτ∼πθ[∑t=0T−1γtrt].J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta} \left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t r_t\right].J(θ)=Eτ∼πθ​​[t=0∑T−1​γtrt​].

This averages over trajectories. Initial conditions, disturbances, and the randomness of the policy all affect the return, so I would want to look at several runs rather than pick a successful one.

The Markov assumption means the state contains the information needed to predict the next-state distribution given an action. A camera frame often does not: it may hide velocity, occluded objects, or whether something is slipping. In practice I may need observation history, a state estimator, or a recurrent policy:

at∼πθ(⋅∣o0:t,a0:t−1).a_t\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid o_{0:t},a_{0:t-1}).at​∼πθ​(⋅∣o0:t​,a0:t−1​).

That is a partially observed problem, even if the simulator has access to a complete state. Spinning Up is a useful reference for the notation; these notes use a finite episode unless stated otherwise.

Choosing a reward

Suppose a one-dimensional end effector must reach a target x⋆x^\starx⋆. A deliberately simple reward is

rt=−wx(xt−x⋆)2−wvvt2−wuut2.r_t=-w_x(x_t-x^\star)^2-w_v v_t^2-w_u u_t^2.rt​=−wx​(xt​−x⋆)2−wv​vt2​−wu​ut2​.

The first term rewards accuracy; the second discourages moving quickly near the target; the third penalizes control effort. The weights specify how much each behavior matters. Their units and relative scales affect the solution. A large effort penalty can make doing very little preferable to completing the task.

A sparse success reward says clearly what I want, but may give little guidance before the first success. A dense reward provides guidance, but can favor an unintended shortcut. I should test the reward on deliberately bad behaviors: hovering near an object, exploiting contact, moving without finishing, or terminating early.

I would keep the actuator limits outside the reward function. A torque penalty makes large commands less attractive during optimization; a hard limit actually bounds what reaches the motor. The controller still needs termination conditions and a reliable way to stop the robot.

Discounting and the control timestep

For an infinite stream of unit rewards, the discounted sum is 1/(1−γ)1/(1-\gamma)1/(1−γ) when 0≤γ<10\leq\gamma<10≤γ<1. That gives a rough horizon measured in steps. To relate it to the robot, I need to include the duration of each step.

If I want an exponential discount time constant τd\tau_dτd​ in seconds, a useful parameterization is

γ=exp⁡(−Δt/τd).\gamma=\exp(-\Delta t/\tau_d).γ=exp(−Δt/τd​).

If I double the control frequency and leave every other setting unchanged, I have changed more than the number of actions. I may also have changed the effective horizon, the reward accumulated per second, and the meaning of a smoothness penalty. For a reward intended as a continuous-time rate, multiplying it by Δt\Delta tΔt is part of a consistent discretization.

Values and advantages

The state value is expected future return from a state under the policy. The action value additionally fixes the first action:

Vπ(s)=Eπ[Gt∣st=s],Qπ(s,a)=Eπ[Gt∣st=s,at=a].V^\pi(s)=\mathbb E_\pi[G_t\mid s_t=s],\qquad Q^\pi(s,a)=\mathbb E_\pi[G_t\mid s_t=s,a_t=a].Vπ(s)=Eπ​[Gt​∣st​=s],Qπ(s,a)=Eπ​[Gt​∣st​=s,at​=a].

For a stationary discounted problem, the Bellman relation is

Qπ(s,a)=E[rt+γ Ea′∼π(⋅∣s′)Qπ(s′,a′)].Q^\pi(s,a)=\mathbb E\left[r_t+ \gamma\,\mathbb E_{a'\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s')}Q^\pi(s',a')\right].Qπ(s,a)=E[rt​+γEa′∼π(⋅∣s′)​Qπ(s′,a′)].

In a finite-horizon task, remaining time belongs in the state or the value function needs a time index. At a true terminal state, there is no future reward to bootstrap. An artificial time-limit truncation is not automatically the same thing as task termination.

The advantage compares an action with the policy's average expectation at that state:

Aπ(s,a)=Qπ(s,a)−Vπ(s).A^\pi(s,a)=Q^\pi(s,a)-V^\pi(s).Aπ(s,a)=Qπ(s,a)−Vπ(s).

The actor uses these estimates to adjust the policy. Errors in the critic then affect which actions the update encourages, which is one reason value learning deserves as much attention as the policy network.

PPO

PPO collects experience using a policy and then optimizes a surrogate objective. For the clipped version, define the probability ratio

ηt(θ)=πθ(at∣ot)πθold(at∣ot).\eta_t(\theta)= \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid o_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(a_t\mid o_t)}.ηt​(θ)=πθold​​(at​∣ot​)πθ​(at​∣ot​)​.

The policy objective is

Lclip(θ)=Et[min⁡(ηtA^t,clip⁡(ηt,1−ϵ,1+ϵ)A^t)].L^{\rm clip}(\theta)=\mathbb E_t\left[ \min\left(\eta_t\hat A_t, \operatorname{clip}(\eta_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat A_t\right) \right].Lclip(θ)=Et​[min(ηt​A^t​,clip(ηt​,1−ϵ,1+ϵ)A^t​)].

The clipping limits the incentive for certain large probability-ratio changes in this objective. It does not impose a hard bound on the robot’s motion or guarantee a small change in behavior. A practical implementation also needs value learning and choices about entropy, normalization, batching, and advantage estimation. The original PPO paper is the right place to check the actual objective.

PPO is primarily on-policy: it relies on recently collected experience from the behavior policy used for the update. Off-policy methods such as Soft Actor-Critic can reuse experience through a replay buffer. SAC also includes an entropy term in its objective. The ability to reuse data is attractive when collecting robot experience is expensive. Whether it helps enough on a particular setup still needs to be measured.

A reaching example

Before thinking about a humanoid, consider a one-dimensional point mass:

vt+1=vt+utΔt,xt+1=xt+vt+1Δt.v_{t+1}=v_t+u_t\Delta t,\qquad x_{t+1}=x_t+v_{t+1}\Delta t.vt+1​=vt​+ut​Δt,xt+1​=xt​+vt+1​Δt.

An action changes velocity, and velocity changes position. Being close to the target is not enough: arriving with large velocity can mean immediately overshooting it.

Moving from simulation to hardware

Simulation makes repeated trials practical. It also supplies assumptions about friction, mass, contact, actuator response, sensor quality, and timing. A policy can perform well under those assumptions and fail when one of them changes.

One approach is to train across randomized environments:

J(θ)=Eξ∼p(ξ)[J(θ;ξ)],J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{\xi\sim p(\xi)} \left[J(\theta;\xi)\right],J(θ)=Eξ∼p(ξ)​[J(θ;ξ)],

where ξ\xiξ could represent masses, friction coefficients, delays, or observation noise. The choice of distribution matters. Randomizing friction in a narrow range will not tell me much about a surface far outside that range.

Tobin et al. studied visual domain randomization for simulation-to-real transfer. For a control example, Solving Rubik's Cube with a Robot Hand describes automatic domain randomization in a real robotic manipulation system. The robot-hand work is useful here because it connects the training distribution to a concrete hardware problem.

Back at the robot

Before trying a policy on hardware, I would go back through the observation and action definitions, check the timing, and test what happens when the measurements are noisy or delayed. I would also evaluate task completion separately from reward. It is possible to improve the number being optimized without making the behavior I care about more reliable.

What interests me is how much of the eventual behavior comes from the learned policy and how much comes from the rest of the setup: the low-level controller, the reward, the reset distribution, and the simulator. Those choices are easy to leave out of a short explanation of RL, but they are part of the experiment.
